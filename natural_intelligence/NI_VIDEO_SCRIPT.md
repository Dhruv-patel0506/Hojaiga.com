# Natural Intelligence: explainer video script and production brief

Version 1, 15 November 2026. Written to be handed, whole, to a video-making AI (for example Gemini).

"Natural Intelligence" is a working name. Another company already uses it, so the final product will be renamed.

---

## PART A. Instructions for the video-making AI (read before making anything)

You are making an explainer video about a system called **Natural Intelligence (NI)**. It must be accurate to the creator's idea. Follow these rules exactly.

**Format**
- **Length:** about 12 minutes for the full version (Part C, 17 scenes). A 60-second version is in Part D.
- **Style:** clean, modern, technical but warm. Dark navy background, white text, one accent colour for NI (teal) and one for the other AIs (grey-blue). Smooth motion graphics, simple shapes, no clutter.
- **Voice:** calm, confident, curious. Not hype. Speak like a good teacher explaining something important to a smart friend.
- **Music:** low, steady, slightly hopeful. Never louder than the voice.

**Accuracy rules (do not break these)**
1. **Use the narration text as written.** You may shorten a sentence for timing, but never add claims, numbers or promises.
2. **Keep "built" and "proposed" separate.** When the script says something is proposed, the video must show it as a plan, not as working.
3. **Use the numbers exactly as written.** Do not round them differently or invent new ones.
4. **Do not use these words or ideas:** "conscious", "sentient", "feels", "quantum", "deterministic", "replaces humans", "handles anything", "AGI".
5. **Do not show real logos or characters.** The Marvel-style picture (Part B, item 1) is an inspiration only: draw original, generic AI figures (glowing shapes, robots without faces), never Marvel characters or logos. Show company names only as plain text if needed, never their logos.
6. **NI does not have feelings.** When the script talks about tone or emotion, show it as NI *reading* a situation (like gauges and signals), never as NI *feeling*.

## PART B. The idea in plain words (background for you, the video maker)

1. **The picture that started it.** In the Marvel story, Tony Stark's AIs (JARVIS, Vision, Ultron, FRIDAY) lived under one roof. Some were on opposite sides, yet each simply knew the others existed and had different strengths. AIs do not feel rivalry. NI is the intelligence that lives **above** all of them.
2. **The company picture.** A company has layers: board, chief executive, finance, technology, managers, workers, interns. Every layer is controlled by the one above it. AI agents today have workers and no management. NI is the **management layer for AI workers**.
3. **The one-sentence idea.** NI learns from the real outcomes of AI agents whom to trust with what, keeps every alternative alive until the evidence picks one, and tells humans when and why they are needed.
4. **What makes it different.** Today's AIs are workers that answer. NI is the judgement above the workers. It does not try to out-reason them. It decides which one to trust, when, and what is missing.
5. **What it is not:**
   - **Not a router**, which always sends images to one model and code to another.
   - **Not a chat room** where agents talk to agents.
   - **Not another model** trained once on a big dataset.
   - **Not a JARVIS clone**, a voice assistant on top of a rented model.

### Scene 1. The hook (0:00 to 0:40)

**Visuals.** A dark room fills with glowing AI figures, each a different colour and shape. Each one is working alone: writing code, browsing, answering questions. Speech bubbles overlap. No one is in charge. A small red spark appears: one figure is about to press a "delete" button.

**On-screen text.** "Many AIs. No manager."

**Narration.**
> Today you can hire a dozen artificial intelligences in an afternoon. One writes code. One searches the web. One answers your email. Each of them is brilliant at what it was built for.
>
> But ask a simple question: who is in charge of them? Who knows which one is right today, which one got worse after last night's update, and when a human has to step in?
>
> Right now, the answer is nobody.

>
> An agent can be right on Monday and wrong on Tuesday, after its maker updates it, and nothing notices. And the permissions we give agents were designed for humans: all or nothing.

### Scene 5. Two brains: the outer brain and the inner brain (3:10 to 4:10)

**Visuals.** Two concentric rings around a central point.
- **The outer ring** shows four small voting figures labelled "logic", "pattern", "memory", "explorer". They vote and produce an answer.
- **The inner ring** watches the outer ring and shows a gauge labelled "Is this answer likely right?".

A small panel appears with numbers (Built).

**On-screen text.**
- "Outer brain: forms a judgement."
- "Inner brain: judges the judgement."
- Panel: "Built: 85.5% average accuracy on 41 public datasets. Inner brain beats raw confidence: 0.88 vs 0.82."

**Narration.**
> Inside NI there are two layers, like an outer brain and an inner brain.
>
> The outer brain forms a judgement. Four different kinds of predictor vote: one reasons with simple statistics, one finds patterns, one remembers similar past cases, and one explores. No single opinion decides.
>
> The inner brain does something today's AIs don't do well. It judges the judgement. It asks: is this answer likely to be right? Is this agent trustworthy here, for this kind of task? Have I ever seen a situation like this before?
>
> This part is built and measured. Across forty-one public datasets of text, images, audio, video and graphs, the outer brain averages eighty-five point five percent accuracy on data it never saw. And the inner brain predicts its mistakes better than raw confidence does: zero point eight eight against zero point eight two.

### Scene 6. Five possible verdicts (4:10 to 4:50)

**Visuals.** An AI worker raises its hand: "I want to run this command." NI's teal layer lights up, and five buttons appear, each lighting up in turn:
1. **Proceed** (green)
2. **Verify first** (yellow)
3. **Try another agent** (blue)
4. **Ask the human, and say why** (purple)
5. **Stop** (red)

**On-screen text.** "Every step gets a verdict."

**Narration.**
> Before any AI takes a step, NI gives it one of five verdicts. Proceed. Verify first. Hand it to another agent. Ask the human, and explain why. Or stop.
>
> It weighs how likely the step is to succeed, what is gained if it does, and what is lost if it fails. A step that can't be undone, like deleting, paying or sending, costs far more when it goes wrong. So it needs far more confidence, or a human.

### Scene 7. Learning from real outcomes, not from GPU training (4:50 to 5:50)

**Visuals.** A timeline of events flows past: each event is a small card ("action", "agent", "result: passed / failed", "human reaction"). Each card drops into a jar labelled "experience". A belief bar for each agent moves a little with every card. Red cards (failures) are not thrown away. They go into a separate shelf labelled "lessons", each with a tag ("wrong fact", "wrong scope", "too slow", "unsafe").

**On-screen text.**
- "Learns from every outcome. No retraining."
- "Failures are kept as lessons, not thrown away."
- Panel: "Built: about 18,000 real AI agent actions harvested on the creator's computer."

**Narration.**
> Most AI learns once, on huge machines, from a fixed dataset. NI learns differently. Its dataset is its own life.
>
> Every time an agent acts, NI records what was attempted, which agent did it, what really happened, and how the human reacted. Each outcome nudges NI's beliefs about that agent, for that kind of task, in a fraction of a millisecond, on an ordinary computer. There is no retraining.
>
> To be precise: NI still learns from data. Every learning system does. The difference is that it learns continuously, from real results, instead of once from a frozen collection.
>
> And it does not throw failures away. A failure is a lesson: where it happened, why, and to whom. Something that failed in one situation may be exactly right in another. Mature people remember their mistakes together with the circumstances. NI does the same.
>
> So far, NI has learned from about eighteen thousand real actions taken by AI agents on its creator's computer.

**Narration.**

>

>
> But some actions never become automatic, whatever the record: paying, deleting, publishing, sending. Those always go to a human.
>
> NI already keeps a weekly report card like this for every AI agent on its creator's machine.

### Scene 9. Why humans stay needed, in numbers (6:40 to 7:30)

**Visuals.** NI pauses an action and shows a message card to a human:

> "I need you here. The main reasons:
> the payment cannot be undone (41%),
> the two agents disagree on the amount (27%),
> you were right on 9 of 10 contracts like this (18%)."

Behind the card, small gauges show "stakes", "reversibility", "agent agreement", "novelty", "deadline", "your track record".

**On-screen text.**
- "NI asks only when your answer is worth your time."
- Panel: "Built: when NI asks a human, the human was really needed 35% of the time, against 21% for a simple risk threshold."

**Narration.**
> Most systems either ask you about everything, or about nothing. NI asks when your answer is actually worth your time. It does this by measuring the value of the information you would give.
>
> It looks at many factors: how much is at stake, whether the action can be undone, whether the agents agree, whether the situation is new, how close the deadline is, and how often you have been right in cases like this.
>
> And when it asks, it tells you why, with the share of each reason. It does not praise itself or flatter you. It explains why a human is needed in this situation.
>
> In its first measurements, when NI asked a human, the human really was needed thirty-five percent of the time, compared with twenty-one percent for a simple risk threshold. That number has to keep rising, and it is measured.

### Scene 10. Keep every branch alive: the "second child" (7:30 to 8:40)

**Visuals.** A task in the centre ("write this piece of code"). Four branches grow from it, one per AI agent. Each branch runs inside its own transparent box labelled "copy". The original file sits untouched in a glass case with a lock and the label "original never changed". Hidden tests light up each branch: some fully green, some partly red. NI picks the fully green branch and shows a "proof" panel: an evidence table, the losing branches with their reasons, and "what would change my mind".

**On-screen text.**
- "Never prune on a guess."
- "Every branch runs on a copy."
- Panel: "Built: 12 real coding tasks, 4 AI agents. NI shipped 12 of 12 correct, 0 broken. Trusting the most confident agent: 10 of 12. A random single agent: 9.75 of 12."

**Narration.**
> When there is a choice, most systems pick one path and throw the others away. NI keeps every branch alive until the evidence decides. That includes the second child, the option that looked weaker at first.
>
> Each agent's attempt runs on its own copy, never on the original. NI tests every branch, compares them on what was actually measured, and keeps the losers too, with the reason each one lost. Then it recommends one, with the proof attached: the evidence, the alternatives, and what would have changed its mind.
>
> We tested this on twelve real coding tasks with four AI agents. NI shipped the correct answer twelve times out of twelve, and never shipped broken code. Trusting the most confident agent got ten of twelve. Picking an agent at random got nine point seven five on average.
>
> It is an early result. Against a random agent the difference is statistically significant. Against the most confident agent it is not yet, because the two disagreed on only two tasks. More tasks are being run.

### Scene 11. Cross-questioning (8:40 to 9:10)

**Visuals.** Two AI figures disagree (two different answers on screen). NI draws a line between them and asks each: "Defend your answer against the other's evidence." Then NI turns to the human with one short question: "Speed or compactness for this one?"

**On-screen text.** "It asks the one question that matters most."

**Narration.**
> NI also cross-questions. When two agents disagree, it makes each one face the other's evidence, and it runs the disagreement on a copy.
>
> When the human's request is unclear, NI doesn't guess and jump in. It asks for the missing part of the story. And it asks only the single question whose answer would most change the decision. If the options are practically equal, it doesn't bother you at all.

**Status for the video:** the human cross-question is built inside the branch engine. Cross-questioning between agents is **proposed**. Show the agent debate with a small "planned" tag.

**On-screen text.**

- "Proposed."

**Narration.**
> Now imagine a living, gluey layer that can attach to any AI from the outside and take its shape.
>
> If the AI is open source, NI can read how it is built. If it is closed, NI can still learn how it behaves from its answers: what it is good at, where it fails, how sure it really is, and how it changes over time. It does this the way you learn a colleague's character without ever seeing their neurons. There is even a formula for how many test questions are needed before a claim about an agent can be trusted: about seven hundred and thirty-eight per kind of task, to know its success rate within five points.
>
> Whatever the wrapper improves, it improves on a copy. The original is never changed. A change is promoted only after it is tested and a human approves it.
>
> This wrapper is the next stage. The copy-on-write safety rule underneath it is already built.

## PART E. Glossary: every key word explained

Show these as small captions when the word first appears, or use them to keep the visuals accurate.

| Word | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Agent** | An AI that does things, not just talks: it runs commands, edits files, browses, clicks. |
| **Outcome** | What really happened after an action: it worked, it failed, the human undid it, or the human corrected it. |
| **Outer brain** | The part of NI that forms a judgement by combining several different predictors. |
| **Inner brain** | The part of NI that judges the outer brain's judgement: "is this likely right?" |
| **Verdict** | NI's decision about one step: proceed, verify, try another agent, ask the human, or stop. |
| **Irreversible** | Cannot be undone: paying, deleting, publishing, sending. These always need a human. |
| **Earned autonomy** | How much freedom an agent has, based on its real track record. It goes up and down. |
| **Drift** | When an agent's behaviour changes, usually after its maker updates it. NI detects it. |
| **Value of information** | How much a human's answer would improve the decision, compared with the cost of their time. |
| **Branch** | One alternative way to do a task, often from a different agent. |
| **Copy-on-write** | Every change happens on a copy. The original is never touched until a human approves. |
| **Calibration** | Whether "90% sure" really means right 90% of the time. NI checks and repairs its own. |
| **Hallucination** | A confident claim or connection that is not true. NI keeps a bridge only if it improves a real result. |
| **Wrapper** | The proposed layer that attaches to any AI from outside and learns how it behaves. |
| **Hard values** | Rules never traded for speed or money: safety, consent, privacy, law. |
